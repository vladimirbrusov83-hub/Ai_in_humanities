Before you start

Three words, and a spectrum

Hermeneutic circle

In plain words: you can’t understand the whole without the parts, or the parts without the whole — so you go round and round, and each pass changes the last one. Reading a chapter changes what the first chapter meant.

Positionality

In plain words: where you’re standing when you look. Your language, your training, your century, what you owe to whom. In much of the humanities this isn’t bias to remove — it’s part of the method, and you’re expected to say it out loud.

Historiography

In plain words: not what happened, but the story of how scholars have argued about what happened — and why the argument changed when it did.

Critical AI literacy

In plain words: being able to understand these systems, use them where they help, and judge them — including their labour, environmental and epistemic costs. ACRL’s AI Competencies for Academic Library Workers (approved October 2025) is explicit that this is not a duty to adopt: “AI literacy should not imply an obligation to indiscriminately adopt AI tools for all purposes.”

AI use is a spectrum, not a verdict

Where a scholar sits on this line is a defensible position at every point, including the far left. Departments, journals and supervisors draw the line in different places, and this page does not draw it for you. Each position below is anchored to a named source, so you can go and argue with the argument rather than with a label.

Reject

No AI at any stage. A coherent position: the labour is the contribution.

Refusal: Sano-Franchini, Fernandes & McIntyre, Refusing Generative AI in Writing Studies (2024) — these technologies are “not ideologically neutral,” and refusal is a disciplinary position, not a failure to keep up.
Bounded & skeptical

Mechanical tasks only — OCR cleanup, formatting, a translation triage — everything verified, everything disclosed.

Cautious integration: MLA–CCCC Joint Task Force on Writing and AI, Working Paper 1 (2023, cochairs Holly Hassel and Elizabeth Losh) — build critical AI literacy through ethical, mission-driven policy rather than prohibition.
Integrated tool

Routine across drafting, structure and search, with the interpretive claims kept firmly in your own hands.

Disciplinary guidance: American Historical Association, Guiding Principles for AI in History Education (approved 29 July 2025) — banning is impractical, AI literacy is teachable, generative AI regularly fabricates sources, and every syllabus needs an explicit policy.
AI-first

The model drafts and the scholar edits. Rare in the humanities, and in tension with what a monograph certifies.

No named advocate: no humanities disciplinary body currently endorses this position. Its defenders are individual practitioners, mostly in computational and digital-humanities work.

Who this map fits, and where it strains. It was drawn for text-based interpretive research, so it fits literature, history, philosophy, religious studies and languages closely; art history and the interpretive social sciences fit it with substitutions (the object, the site, the interview transcript in place of the text); and quantitative social science and practice-based arts research strain it hardest, because neither has a phase here for the survey instrument or for making the work itself. Those are the two places the stress-test tool at the bottom gets the most corrections, and they are corrections we want.

Three phases send you backward. Close reading (04) sends you back to the research question (01). Scholarly dialogue (08) sends you back to the sources (03). And the afterlife (10) opens the question again at 01, for you or for someone else. Every card below carries a gold badge where that happens.

What “agentic” actually means

One request, many helpers

A chatbot answers a question and stops. An agentic tool takes a whole job, splits it into parts, hands each part to a helper, then looks at what came back and runs it again. Here is what that looks like in three humanities tasks — and what still has to come back to you.

03 · Archival Engagement

“Make this scanned collection readable.”

One assistant takes the request and splits it up
runs OCR over each page image cleans up the garbled words drafts a rough translation of each page
It re-runs the pages that came back poorly.

Back to you: every “cleaned up” word, checked against the page image. A smooth transcription can be a confident invention.

04 · Close Reading

“Find every place this image appears across the corpus.”

One assistant takes the request and splits it up
searches the texts for the pattern pulls each passage with its context groups them and counts
Each new pattern you spot sends it round again.

Back to you: the reading. It found the passages; what they mean is the part nobody can hand off.

05 · Citation Verification

“Put this bibliography into Chicago.”

One assistant takes the request and splits it up
reformats each entry to the style flags entries missing a field checks the list for inconsistencies
It fixes what it flagged and checks again.

Back to you: whether each source exists. Formatting is safe to delegate; existence never is.

What none of it does: decide whether the question was worth asking, read a text from somewhere in particular, or answer for the claim once it is in print. That stays with you.

The floor an agent can’t reach

Four things only the library can do

Not a services list — structural capabilities no model has, by construction. In the humanities this floor sits higher than it does in the sciences, because the evidence itself is held, described and preserved by libraries and archives.

01

Archival & special collections access

The material that was never digitised

A model can only reason over text that was scanned, transcribed and put online — a thin, uneven and heavily Anglophone slice of the record. The manuscript, the letter, the unprocessed box, the object: these are reachable because an institution holds them and a person will bring them to your table.

Looks like: an archivist saying “the finding aid doesn’t mention it, but the related correspondence is in a different collection upstairs.”

02

Primary-source discovery & authentication

Is this what it claims to be?

Provenance, edition, translation history, forgery, the difference between a reprint and the 1798 printing. Authentication is a chain of institutional custody, not an inference from a document’s contents — and it is the thing a model has no access to at all.

Looks like: learning the edition you cited was silently bowdlerised, and the line you built a chapter on isn’t in the original.

03

Citation & historiographical expertise

Verification with standing

A librarian can confirm a source exists, find the scholarship that was never indexed, chase a footnote back through fifty years, and tell you which edition your field actually cites. When a citation is questioned in review, a verification from the library is a determination; an AI’s confidence is not.

Looks like: the article you couldn’t find turning out to be real, in German, under a different title, in a 1966 Festschrift.

04

Research consultation & instruction

A person who asks you a better question

The highest-leverage hour in a humanities project is usually an appointment with a subject librarian who knows the field, the collections and what you haven’t thought to ask. That is a relationship with an institution, held over years, and it is not an information-retrieval problem.

Looks like: walking in with a topic and walking out with a question, three collections and a name to email.

None of the four narrows as models improve, because none of them is a capability gap. They are questions about custody, standing and relationship.

“But the library is adding AI too.” True — Scopus AI, the Primo and Web of Science research assistants, JSTOR and EBSCO all ship one now. They are retrieval-backed, which narrows fabrication but does not remove it, and they inherit the corpus they sit on: indexes built for the sciences, weighted to journal articles, English, and recent years. That is close to the exact inverse of a CAHSS evidence base, which runs on monographs, edited volumes, non-English scholarship and unindexed primary material. Teaching you to evaluate those tools is a library service; it is not a substitute for the four capabilities above.

Two things to take with you

Use the map, then argue with it

The first tool writes the disclosure you may be required to provide. The second asks you to find where this map is wrong for your field.

AI Disclosure Statement Generator

Many journals, presses and supervisors now require a statement of AI use. A good one is specific about the task, names the verification, and states the boundary — what you did not use it for. Fill in four fields.

Check your target’s actual policy before submitting — wording requirements vary, and some venues want the statement in a specific section. Two things are close to settled across venues: an AI tool cannot be listed as an author (COPE, Authorship and AI tools, 2023, joined by WAME and JAMA), because authorship carries accountability a tool cannot hold; and if you cite the tool rather than only disclose it, Chicago 18th ed. credits it in a note and usually keeps it out of the bibliography, MLA treats your prompt as the title and does not name the AI as author, and APA dropped its version-date convention in 2025. Style rules here have changed twice in two years — confirm against the current manual.

Stress-test this map

This structure was adapted from a model built for the sciences, by people who are not standing where you are standing. It will be wrong somewhere for your discipline. Mark where, and take the result to your seminar, your librarian, or back to us.

The loop doesn’t close

Interpretation is never finished, and that is the point.

AI can clean a scan, format a footnote and draft a summary, and that is worth having. It cannot stand anywhere, owe anyone anything, or be accountable for a claim in print. The library holds the material that makes interpretation possible and keeps it reachable for readings nobody has had yet. What happens in between is yours.
